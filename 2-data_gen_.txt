# Dataset generation

## Sampling and the non-overlap condition

In the _Ribbon_ code, both radius $R$ and pitch $P$ may assume positive, negative, or
  zero values, but $R \neq P \neq 0$. For right handed helices, $P > 0$, and
for left handed helices $P < 0$. If $R<0$, the NPL simply turns inside out,
while preserving the handedness. If $R = 0$ (at $P \neq 0$), the helical NPL
degenerates to a _helicoid_. If $P = 0$ (at $R \neq 0$), the helical NPL
degenerates to a _cylinder_. If $R = \pm \infty$ and $P = \pm \infty$, the helical NPL
degenerates to a _flat_ configuration. 

In order to avoid adding polydispersity in shape and size, we stick to
  helical ribbons, i.e. $R \neq 0$, $P \neq 0$ and finite. To aid in intuitive
clarity and facilitate interpretation of experimental data, we will add the
restriction that $R > 0$ as well (assuming that turning an NPL inside out makes
no difference).

To prevent overlap (or penetration), $R$ and $P$ _may not_ be chosen
  independently. There are several restrictions depending on the length $L$ and
width $W$ of the NPL (the thickness does not matter for helical NPLs). Thus
when sampling conformations $R$ and $P$ are not independent random variables;
we need to take into account the conditional probability distribution.

The criterion for $R$ is that $ W/2\pi < R \leq \infty$. Within those bounds on
$R$, if $R \geq L/2\pi$, any pitch may be chosen, i.e. $R$ and $P$ become
independent random variables. If $ W/2\pi < R < L/2\pi$, $P \geq W/\cos \psi$,
where $\psi$ is the helix angle. The plot below shows the minimum value of
$\lvert P \rvert$ as a function of $R$ at $L = 100$nm and $W = 10$ nm.  The
plot ends where $R$ and $P$ become independent. At all points the sampled $P$
must have an absolute value lying on or above the curve.

:::{figure} images/fig-allowed-pitch.png
:width: 600px
:align: center

Allowable values of pitch $\lvert P \rvert_{\mathrm{min}}$ for $L = 100$ nm and
$W = 10$ nm.
:::

Three distributions are considered in the code: (1) Uniform, (2) Normal, (3) Lognormal.
  To ensure positivity, we use lognormal for $R$, though uniform may also be
used. For pitch, the distribution is normal, but truncated with a lower bound
dependent on $R$. During sampling, we first choose $R$ from a lognormal
distribution with a location parameter $W/2\pi$. Then we calculate $\delta$ and
sample $P$ from the truncated normal distribution using the inverse transform
of the CDF.

## Creating the data set

We sample the parameters of the respective distributions uniformly. For a
uniform distribution, the mean $\bar{x}$ is sampled uniformly within provided
bounds, as is the polydispersity PD. In this case PD = $\sigma/{\bar{x}}$, were
uniform distribution has bounds $(\bar{x}-\sigma, \bar{x}+\sigma)$. For normal
distribution, $\sigma$ is the usual standard deviation. For lognormal, $\sigma$
is not used. Following the parametrization of particle sizes in the literature,
$\bar{x}$ is the median, rather than the mean and $PD >1$ is the _geometric
standard deviation_. Thus, 68.27\% of values fall within $[\bar{x}/\mathrm{PD},
\bar{x} \mathrm{PD}]$.

### Choosing the range of polydispersity

We supply just the bounds on $\bar{x}$ and PD, rest is done within the code.
We use $\bar{R}, \bar{P} \in [5, 50]$ nm. The next issue is how to choose the
bounds on PD, given the fact that a sufficiently large value will smooth out
the SAXS pattern and leave it featureless. The plots below compare SAXS
patterns for 2ML NPLs at different values of PD at fixed mean values of $R$ and
$P$.

### Choosing the number of NPLs within the volume

Having chosen the parameters of the distributions, the question arises of how
many _samples_ (NPLs) for _each distribution_. In dilute solution, the SAXS
pattern is the average over each particle in the solution volume under
consideration.  Formally, we write the expression for the differential
scattering cross-section per unit volume as

\begin{equation}
I(Q) \propto \frac{1}{V} \frac{d\Sigma(Q)}{d\Omega} 
= \frac{1}{V} \sum_k^N \biggl [ 
    \sum_{i,j} f_i(Q) f_j(Q) \frac{\sin Qr_{ij}}{Qr_{ij}} 
    \biggr ],
\end{equation}

where the summation over $k$ goes over all NPLs $N$ in $V$ and the sum over
$i,j$ goes over all atoms of an NPL. The factors $f_i(Q)$ are calculated by
_debyer_ in units of electrons (dimensionless). We multiply by the
electron scattering length $r_e$ = 2.818 fm to keep the above equation
dimensionally consistent. Assuming a volume fraction of 0.1\%, $\phi = 0.001$
and that all NPLs have the same volume $v_p$ (the code does not assume equal
volumes) the above equation can be rewritten as 

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{V} \frac{d\Sigma(Q)}{d\Omega} = r_e^2 \frac{\phi}{\sum_k v_p} P(Q) 
 =  r_e^2 \frac{0.001}{N v_p} P(Q).
\end{equation}

The LHS has dimension cm$^{-1}$. Now we need to figure out how to choose $N$.
_debyer_ will run once for each $N$, times the number of samples of
distributions, so it should not be too large. On the other hand, too small a
value will lead to undersampling, especially for higher polydispersities.

The plot below shows the effect of increasing $N$ on the SAXS patterns for $L =
100$ nm, $W=10$nm, $\bar{R}=10$ nm, PD$_R$ = 1.2, $\bar{P}=30$ nm, and PD$_P$ =
1.2. Here, $R$ is from a lognormal distribution and $P$ from a normal
distribution.

### Choosing the Q-spacing

In the plots before we have used a $Q$-spacing of 0.01 nm$^{-1}$.
Even for 2ML NPLs, going upto $Q = 80$ nm$^{-1}$ is computationally challenging
if we persist with the $Q$ spacing used before. Here I compare _split
spacings_: $\Delta Q = 0.01$ nm$^{-1}$ for $Q \in (0.01, 1)$ nm$^{-1}$
and $\Delta Q = 0.1$ nm$^{-1}$ for $Q \in (1, 80)$ nm$^{-1}$ _vs_

### Workflow for creating the dataset

We intend to create $\approx 6000$ samples to be split into 70-15-15\% for
training, validation, and testing, respectively.

To generate each sample for 2ML CdSe NPLs, we follow the following wokflow:

1. Choose the parameters of the distribution function from the supplied range.

2. For each parameter set, calculate the SAXS pattern for $N = 256$ NPLs and
   sum them over with appropriate prefactors.

3. These samples are labeled by the parameters of the distributions and saved
   to a _numpy_ compressed zip file.

The dataset thus prepared will be fed to a VAE-based neural network in PyTorch
to train it.
